THE COURT: We are on the record in United States versus Elena Marsh, case number 24-CR-118. Counsel, please state your appearances.

MS. FENWICK: Good afternoon, Your Honor. Claire Fenwick for the United States.

MR. GALLOWAY: Good afternoon. Peter Galloway for Ms. Marsh, who is present beside me.

THE COURT: Good afternoon to you both. Ms. Marsh, I understand you wish to plead guilty to Count Two of the indictment. Before I can accept that plea, I need to ask you some questions under oath. Madam Clerk, please swear the defendant.

THE CLERK: Please raise your right hand. Do you solemnly swear that the answers you are about to give will be the truth, the whole truth, and nothing but the truth?

THE DEFENDANT: I do.

THE COURT: Please state your full name and spell your last name for the record.

THE DEFENDANT: Elena Rose Marsh. M-A-R-S-H.

THE COURT: How old are you, Ms. Marsh, and how far did you go in school?

THE DEFENDANT: I'm 42. I finished two years of community college.

THE COURT: Have you taken any medicine, drugs or alcohol in the last 48 hours that might affect your ability to understand what we are doing today?

THE DEFENDANT: No, Your Honor.

THE COURT: Mr. Galloway, do you have any doubt about your client's competence to enter a plea today?

MR. GALLOWAY: None, Your Honor. We have met several times, most recently yesterday for about two hours.

THE COURT: Ms. Marsh, have you had enough time to discuss this case with your lawyer, and are you satisfied with his advice?

THE DEFENDANT: Yes. He explained everything.

THE COURT: You have the right to plead not guilty and to have a trial by jury. At that trial you would be presumed innocent, and the government would have to prove your guilt beyond a reasonable doubt. You could see and question the witnesses against you, call your own witnesses, and choose whether or not to testify. If you plead guilty, there will be no trial, and you give up those rights. Do you understand?

THE DEFENDANT: I understand.

THE COURT: Ms. Fenwick, please state the elements of the offense and the maximum penalty.

MS. FENWICK: Yes, Your Honor. Count Two charges wire fraud. The government would have to prove that the defendant knowingly devised a scheme to obtain money by false statements, that the statements were material, and that she used an interstate wire, here an email sent on March 3rd, 2023, to carry out the scheme. The maximum penalty is 20 years in prison, a fine of $250,000, three years of supervised release, restitution, and a $100 special assessment.

THE COURT: Ms. Marsh, do you understand the charge and the maximum penalty?

THE DEFENDANT: Yes, Your Honor.

THE COURT: Has anyone promised you what your sentence will be, or threatened you in any way to make you plead guilty?

THE DEFENDANT: No.

THE COURT: Then tell me in your own words what you did.

THE DEFENDANT: I sent invoices from my company for office supplies that were never delivered, and I knew they were false when I sent them. One of them was the email on March 3rd.

THE COURT: How do you plead to Count Two?

THE DEFENDANT: Guilty.

THE COURT: I find that the plea is knowing and voluntary and supported by an independent basis in fact. The plea is accepted. Sentencing is set for June 14th at 10:00 a.m.
